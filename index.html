Module 03 · haemodynamics primer

Cardiac output, resistance & the shock states

The numbers the LCOS tool leans on — CO/CI, SVR, CVP and SvO₂ — are terms of one equation. Read together they tell you which term has failed, and therefore which shock you are treating.

1 One equation behind it all

MAP − CVP = (CO × SVR) / 80
A low MAP is always a fall in CO, a fall in SVR, or both — referenced against CVP as the downstream pressure. Phenotyping is just asking which term broke.

Rearranged, SVR = (MAP − CVP) × 80 / CO. The whole vasoactive strategy reduces to one choice: defend the pressure term (tone) or the flow term (output).

2 Cardiac output & index

CO = HR × SV, and SV is set by preload, afterload and contractility. CI = CO / BSA lets one threshold (≈2.2 L/min/m²) travel across body sizes — a 2 L/min output is catastrophic in a large adult and generous in a neonate.
LeverBedside handle
Rate / rhythmRestore AV synchrony, rate 80–100 — the atrial kick matters most in the stiff post-op ventricle
PreloadVolume to responsiveness (dynamic, not static CVP); over-fill harms the RV
AfterloadSVR for the LV, PVR for the RV

Indexing distorts at the extremes — BSA over-corrects in obesity (a "low" CI may be adequate) and flatters the cachectic. And CO is only as good as its measurement: thermodilution is thrown by tricuspid regurgitation and shunts, pulse-contour drifts as vasoactives change arterial compliance, and every method assumes a steady state you rarely have after bypass.

3 Systemic vascular resistance

SVR is calculated, not measured — SVR = (MAP − CVP) × 80 / CO — so it inherits every error in MAP, CVP and CO. It is a lumped, steady-state surrogate for afterload; true afterload (arterial elastance / the pressure–volume relationship) includes the pulsatile load and wave reflection SVR ignores.
The distinction that drives agent choice: SVR is the LV's afterload; the RV's afterload is PVR. Defending aortic-root pressure with vasopressin perfuses the RV coronary bed without loading the RV — whereas chasing a "low SVR" number with a pressor in a cold, high-SVR LV just fights the pump. This is the logic behind the phenotype-directed agents in Module 02.

4 Central venous pressure

A poor preload gauge — the CVP–volume relationship is non-linear and shifted by ventricular compliance, tricuspid disease, rhythm, tamponade, PEEP and an open chest, so static CVP predicts fluid responsiveness barely better than chance. It earns its keep two other ways:

Back-pressure (Guyton) — a high CVP narrows the gradient driving venous return and is the congestion that injures kidney and liver. Renal perfusion tracks MAP − CVP, not MAP alone.
Relative to PAOP — CVP approaching or exceeding wedge, with a dilated RV and septal shift, points to RV-predominant failure (feeds the RV phenotype in Module 01).

Why CVP moves the blood pressure
Venous return (Guyton) — VR = (MSFP − CVP) / RVR. CVP is the back-pressure to venous return: at a fixed mean systemic filling pressure, a rising CVP narrows the driving gradient, so less blood returns, preload falls, and CO — hence BP (MAP = CO × SVR) — fall with it.
CVP is an intersection, not a cause — it sits where the venous-return curve meets the cardiac (Starling) curve. A high CVP can mean a full tank (high MSFP) or a failing right heart that cannot move the volume forward. The trace, the trend and the response to a small challenge disambiguate.
Organ perfusion pressure — every mmHg of CVP is subtracted downstream: renal and hepatic perfusion track MAP − CVP. A "normal" MAP over a high CVP still congests and under-perfuses the organ.
The post-op trap — chasing BP with volume when CVP is already high adds congestion without flow (the flat limb of Starling), worsens TR and RV distension, and lowers organ perfusion pressure. Rising CVP + falling BP = right heart / obstruction (RV failure, tamponade, PE, tension) until proven otherwise.

Reading the PA pressure
Mean PA & the threshold — mPAP ≈ ⅓·PASP + ⅔·PADP. Pulmonary hypertension at mPAP ≥ 20 mmHg (≥ 25 by the older definition). A "normal" PA pressure over a high CVP and low output is not reassuring — it can be an RV that can no longer generate PA pressure (see PAPi).
Pre- vs post-capillary — the wedge splits them. PCWP > 15 → post-capillary (LV failure / back-pressure). PCWP ≤ 15 with a high PVR → pre-capillary (pulmonary vascular). Combined disease raises both.
The gradients localise it — TPG = mPAP − PCWP (> 12 abnormal); DPG = PADP − PCWP (> 7 flags a pre-capillary component and is relatively flow- and filling-independent); PVR > 3 Wood units (> 240 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵). A widening PADP−PCWP gap is the pulmonary vasculature, not the left heart.
PAPi & the failing RV — PAPi = (PASP − PADP) / CVP; < 1.0 flags RV failure — the RV cannot mount a PA pulse pressure against its load. Low PA pressures + high CVP + low output is the RV failing to transmit pressure forward, not a benign PA.
Wedge pitfalls
Trust the wedge only where it can be a LAP surrogate: the tip must sit in West zone 3 (below the left atrium, continuous blood column) — in zones 1–2, or under high PEEP, it reads alveolar pressure, not LAP. Read at end-expiration; tall v waves (MR) overestimate mean LAP; use PADP as the surrogate when the wedge is unreliable (DPG normally keeps PADP within a few mmHg of PCWP). Over-wedging risks PA rupture — especially with pulmonary hypertension or anticoagulation — wedge briefly and deflate.
What the PAC feeds the tool: RA/CVP, RV systolic (on the float), PAS / PAD / mean, PCWP, thermodilution CO/CI and SvO₂ — from which the engine derives SVR, PVR, SVI, RVSWI / LVSWI, TPG, DPG and PAPi (see the derived-indices grid in Module 01).

The base to add or remove to titrate blood to pH 7.4 at PaCO₂ 5.3 kPa and 37 °C — a respiratory-independent measure of the metabolic load. Lactate — type A vs type B
Type A (hypoperfusion) — the shock signal: inadequate DO₂, regional or global ischaemia. This is the lactate the LCOS tool treats by raising output and correcting delivery.

Compensation & mixed disorders
Expected respiratory compensation (Winter's): predicted PaCO₂ (mmHg) = 1.5 × HCO₃ + 8 ± 2. A PaCO₂ higher than predicted = a co-existing respiratory acidosis; lower = respiratory alkalosis. The delta ratio (ΔAG / ΔHCO₃) exposes a second metabolic process. The Stewart lens (strong-ion difference, weak acids) explains the saline effect physicochemically — a chloride load lowers the SID and acidifies without any acid being added.
Why the acidosis matters to the circulation
Acidosis degrades the pump — reduced contractility, catecholamine resistance (the reversible-contributor gate in Module 01), pulmonary vasoconstriction that loads the RV, and a lower arrhythmia threshold.
Bicarbonate rarely fixes lactic acidosis — it generates CO₂ (worsening intracellular acidosis unless ventilation rises), lowers ionised Ca²⁺, and shifts the O₂ dissociation curve. Treat the cause (perfusion), not the number; reserve HCO₃⁻ for specific indications (severe hyperchloraemic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, certain toxins).

9 SvO₂ in cardiogenic shock — the delivery–demand balance

Mixed venous saturation is the single number that asks whether the circulation is meeting the body’s needs. It does not measure cardiac output — it measures what is left over after the tissues have taken what they need, which in low output is the more useful question.

What it is, and where it comes from

The relationship inverts in shock. In health ScvO₂ sits slightly below SvO₂ (the kidneys and splanchnic bed return relatively oxygen-rich blood). In shock, flow is redistributed to brain and heart and away from the splanchnic bed, so ScvO₂ typically runs 5–8% above SvO₂. Track one or the other consistently — do not mix them within a trend.
The four determinants — why it falls
Rearranged Fick: SvO₂ ≈ SaO₂ − VO₂ / (CO × Hb × 1.34). There are therefore only four reasons for a low SvO₂ — and the whole management of a low value is deciding which: ↓ CO (the cardiogenic one), ↓ Hb (anaemia), ↓ SaO₂ (hypoxaemia), or ↑ VO₂ (fever, shivering, agitation, work of breathing, thyrotoxicosis, seizure). Fix whichever applies; in post-cardiotomy LCOS it is usually output first, haemoglobin second.
Oxygen extraction ratio — O₂ER = (SaO₂ − SvO₂) / SaO₂, normally 20–30%. A rising O₂ER is the body compensating for falling delivery; above roughly 50–60% extraction is near-maximal and anaerobic metabolism follows. SvO₂ and O₂ER are two views of the same balance. Module 10 computes both

The traps — a “reassuring” SvO₂ that is not
Wedged or over-aspirated sample — drawing from a catheter that has migrated distally, or aspirating too fast, pulls arterialised pulmonary capillary blood and gives a falsely high SvO₂. Aspirate slowly from the distal port, discard the deadspace, and check the waveform first.
Sepsis / mixed shock — microcirculatory shunting means oxygen bypasses the tissues; SvO₂ looks normal or high while lactate climbs. Cardiogenic and septic shock coexist more often than not after cardiotomy.
Reduced demand masks low output — sedation, neuromuscular blockade, hypothermia and mechanical ventilation all cut VO₂, lifting SvO₂ without any improvement in flow. A “normalising” SvO₂ after paralysis is not a haemodynamic win.
Left-to-right shunt — a post-infarct VSD oxygenates right-heart blood directly: a step-up in saturation from RA to PA with a high SvO₂ in a shocked patient. Look for it (§6) rather than being reassured.
On VA-ECMO — the circuit, not the native heart, governs the returned saturation; a PA sample reflects the small native flow and the mixing cloud. Use circuit venous saturation and the targets in the ECMO module instead.
Continuous oximetry drift — fibreoptic PA catheters drift; re-calibrate against a lab co-oximetry sample before acting on an unexpected change, especially a reassuring one.
Never read SvO₂ alone. Pair it with lactate and the CO₂ gap (Pv-aCO₂, normal < 6 mmHg / 0.8 kPa). A normal-to-high SvO₂ with a wide CO₂ gap means flow is still inadequate for metabolic demand despite the reassuring saturation — the combination that most often prevents a missed low-output state. The four-quadrant reading is in the ABG module (§4).
Using it
As a resuscitation target: trend it alongside lactate clearance — a rising SvO₂ with falling lactate is the signature of successful escalation. In weaning: a fall in SvO₂ during an inotrope wean, a trial-off, or a spontaneous breathing trial is an early objective sign the patient is not ready, often preceding any change in blood pressure. Before transfusing: a low SvO₂ with a low Hb is one of the more defensible triggers in a shocked patient, since it demonstrates the anaemia is costing delivery.

2 LV failure management

Recognise: low CI / CPO with a high PAOP and pulmonary congestion, low LVSWI, poor LV on echo (global or regional). Exclude the mechanical mimics first — tamponade, SAM/LVOT obstruction, graft or valve failure — because their management is inverted.
The levers, in order of yield
1 · Afterload reduction — the primary move
LV wall stress rises with afterload, so unloading often does more than adding inotrope. Vasodilators if the BP tolerates; IABP for afterload reduction plus diastolic coronary augmentation; Impella for direct unloading with forward flow. Do not chase MAP with heavy vasoconstriction — it worsens afterload mismatch.
2 · Inotropy
Dobutamine (β₁) or milrinone (inodilator — unloads as it augments, but vasodilates: pair with a vasopressor). Adrenaline for the refractory low-output state. Levosimendan only as a gated rescue adjunct — no survival benefit (LEVO-CTS / CHEETAH / LICORN).
3 · Perfusion pressure
Coronary perfusion of the failing LV ≈ DBP − LVEDP; a high LVEDP and a low DBP starve it. Preserve MAP / DBP with noradrenaline or vasopressin without over-afterloading — the balance is the art.
4 · Preload, rhythm & demand
Optimise (don't maximise) PAOP; restore AV synchrony and rate — the stiff or diastolic-failure LV depends on the atrial kick. Cut demand: sedation/analgesia, treat tachycardia, correct anaemia and temperature.
Escalation to mechanical support
IABP → Impella → VA-ECMO → durable LVAD / bridge, driven by trajectory and CPO. VA-ECMO raises LV afterload — the retrograde arterial flow can distend a failing LV that cannot eject against it, causing pulmonary oedema, stasis and thrombus. Whenever VA-ECMO is used for LV failure, plan an unloading / venting strategy in parallel (IABP, Impella, atrial septostomy, or surgical vent).
Traps: over-vasoconstriction to "fix" the number worsens the failing LV; the unvented LV on VA-ECMO distends silently; and calling low output "LV failure" without excluding tamponade / SAM sends you down an inverted, harmful path.

3 RV failure management — the four levers

Recognise: a high CVP with CVP > PAOP, PAPi <1.0, a dilated / poorly contracting RV, low RVSWI, an under-filled LV and often Kussmaul's sign. Distinguish from tamponade (equalised pressures, blunted y) and from LV failure. Post-cardiotomy drivers: RV stun, coronary air / RCA embolus, protamine reaction, pulmonary hypertension, massive PE, inferior infarction.
Lever 1 · Preload — optimise, never maximise
The failing RV is usually over-filled, not under-filled. Give volume only with dynamic evidence of responsiveness; otherwise offload (diuresis or RRT). Over-filling distends the RV, shifts the septum and impairs the LV.
Lever 2 · Reduce RV afterload (PVR)
The central lever. Aggressively correct the PVR-raisers — hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis, atelectasis, pain. Lung-protective ventilation, avoid over-PEEP and high tidal volumes. Selective pulmonary vasodilators: inhaled nitric oxide or inhaled prostacyclin (iloprost / epoprostenol) — pulmonary effect without systemic hypotension.
Lever 3 · RV contractility
Dobutamine, milrinone (inodilator — augments while lowering PVR), adrenaline for the refractory RV. Milrinone + vasopressin is the preferred pairing: the inodilator supports the RV and drops PVR while vasopressin holds systemic pressure.
Lever 4 · Systemic (coronary) perfusion pressure
The RV free wall is perfused across the aortic-to-RV gradient; when systemic pressure falls, the RV turns ischaemic and the spiral begins. Vasopressin is preferred — it raises SVR (and RV coronary perfusion) while relatively sparing the PVR, unlike phenylephrine or heavy α-agonism. Keep the MAP up to break the spiral.
Rhythm: AV synchrony and the atrial kick matter even more for the failing RV — pace AV-sequential, and treat arrhythmia promptly.
Escalation to mechanical support
RVAD, ProtekDuo (single dual-lumen internal-jugular cannula, ± in-line oxygenator when oxygenation is also failing) or VA-ECMO. Escalate early for the refractory RV rather than piling on pressors into an ischaemic ventricle. (See the VA-ECMO module for the RV four-lever bundle and the LV-distension logic.)
The RV death spiral: hypotension → RV ischaemia → worse RV function → septal shift and worse LV filling → deeper hypotension. Break it deliberately — perfusion pressure (vasopressin), PVR reduction (iNO, correct gases) and inotropy — and do not volume-load the already distended RV.

4 Adrenaline — the lactate / glucose trap & continue-vs-stop

Low-dose adrenaline is a common step for the poor RV (or refractory LV), but it confounds your best perfusion marker: it raises lactate and glucose directly. Reading that lactate as worsening shock — and escalating — is a classic error.

The trap
Adrenaline drives a type-B hyperlactataemia — β₂-mediated glycolysis and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase stimulation generate pyruvate faster than it clears — and simultaneously causes hyperglycaemia (glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, insulin resistance). A lactate that climbs after you start or uptitrate adrenaline, tracking with a rising glucose, is usually the drug, not the shock.

Continue vs stop
Continue if adrenaline delivers genuine benefit — better CI / CPO / SvO₂, MAP held — and the rising lactate is type B. Control the glucose (insulin) and follow SvO₂ / CI / CPO and lactate clearance, not the absolute number.
Stop / wean when the ventricle has recovered (adequate CI / CPO on noradrenaline + milrinone alone), when there is significant tachyarrhythmia or ectopy, or when it buys metabolic / arrhythmic cost without haemodynamic benefit — then swap to a cleaner inotrope or escalate to mechanical support rather than chase the dose.
Positioning & weaning order: adrenaline is a later-line agent — after first-line noradrenaline (perfusion pressure) and the inodilator (milrinone / dobutamine), reserved for the refractory low-output state. It is usually the first catecholamine to come off, leaving noradrenaline + milrinone as the maintenance pairing.

5 LVOT obstruction & SAM — the inverted state

A dynamic gradient in the LV outflow, usually with systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet. Because it is load-dependent, the reflex response to the hypotension it causes — inotropes, fluid restriction, vasodilatation — deepens the obstruction. Recognising it flips the whole management.

What sets it up

The trap (the vicious cycle): obstruction → low stroke volume → hypotension — and the instinct is to add inotrope, cut fluid, vasodilate, each of which worsens the gradient and drops the pressure further. A post-AVR / post-repair patient who is hypotensive and getting worse on inotropes is SAM until proven otherwise.
Recognise
Bedside: hypotension that worsens (or fails to respond) to inotropes; a new, dynamic, harsh systolic ejection murmur. Echo: SAM of the anterior mitral leaflet; a dagger-shaped, late-peaking CW-Doppler LVOT signal; mid-systolic aortic-valve closure/notch; a small hyperdynamic LV with near-cavity obliteration; a posteriorly-directed SAM-related MR jet. The gradient is dynamic — it rises with provocation (Valsalva, post-extrasystolic beat, inotrope) and falls with volume and afterload.
Management — invert every reflex
1 · Stop / cut inotropes
Adrenaline, dobutamine, milrinone, dopamine all deepen the gradient — wean or stop them first.
2 · Fill
Volume enlarges the LV cavity and moves the mitral apparatus off the septum — the obstruction is preload-dependent.
3 · Raise afterload — pure vasoconstrictor
Phenylephrine, noradrenaline or vasopressin. Higher aortic pressure raises LV end-systolic volume and splints the outflow open, cutting the gradient and the SAM-driven MR.
4 · Slow & synchronise
β-blockade (esmolol — titratable) lowers contractility and lengthens diastolic filling; restore sinus / AV synchrony (atrial or AV-sequential pacing — the atrial kick fills the small stiff LV); treat tachyarrhythmia.
Devices & the surgical exit: an IABP is contraindicated — afterload reduction worsens the gradient. If mechanical support is unavoidable, choose one that does not offload the aorta. Definitive fixes are surgical (revision of the mitral repair, septal myectomy) or, in chronic HOCM, alcohol septal ablation. Stop vasodilators (GTN, ACE-inhibitor).

6 RVOT obstruction — dynamic vs fixed

Outflow obstruction has a right-sided twin. It splits into a dynamic (functional, infundibular) form that obeys the same inverted rules as SAM, and a fixed (structural) form that needs a mechanical fix. Getting the two apart — and telling dynamic obstruction from true RV pump failure — decides whether inotropes help or harm.

Dynamic RVOT obstruction — management (invert)
1 · Stop / cut catecholamines & inotropes
Adrenaline, dobutamine, milrinone deepen the infundibular gradient.
2 · Fill the RV
Restore preload — the obstruction is preload-dependent and hypovolaemia is a prime trigger.
3 · Slow & synchronise
Reduce rate; restore AV synchrony; β-blockade (esmolol) once filled to quieten the infundibulum.
4 · Hold coronary / systemic pressure
Noradrenaline or vasopressin — a pure vasoconstrictor perfuses the RV free wall and raises downstream pressure without adding inotropy.
Fixed RVOT obstruction: the fix is mechanical — call the surgeon for a kinked conduit or compressing haematoma, balloon/stent a stenotic conduit, evacuate packing. While you arrange it, maintain preload + a pure vasoconstrictor to hold RV coronary perfusion, and avoid heavy inotropy that only raises demand against an obstruction it cannot clear.
The shared principle
Outflow-tract obstruction inverts the shock reflexes — on either side. Dynamic LVOT (SAM) and dynamic RVOT obstruction are both worsened by inotropes, vasodilators, tachycardia and hypovolaemia, and both treated by volume, pure afterload (vasoconstriction) and rate control / negative inotropy. Two caveats: separate dynamic (medical, inverted) from fixed (mechanical), and separate dynamic obstruction from true pump failure — the inotrope decision is opposite.

14 Oxygenator & circuit health — TMP, post-oxy & the trend

You monitor the oxygenator's decline — you don't wait for it to fail. Four data streams read its health: transmembrane pressure, post-oxygenator gases, haemolysis markers and the consumptive coagulation profile. The trend beats any single value, and every pressure is read at the same flow.

Where the numbers come from
VA-ECMO circuit — pressure taps, sampling ports and the transmembrane gradient
Reading the schematic: a rising ΔP (pre minus post) at fixed flow means resistance inside the bundle — clot. Both pressures rising together with ΔP unchanged is obstruction downstream of the oxygenator, not membrane thrombosis. The access pressure is the negative one — excessively negative means suckdown or a drainage problem, and drives haemolysis. The post-oxygenator port gives the gas that tells you whether the membrane still works; the exhaust port is where a plasma leak appears.
Console labels — what Pint actually is
Pint = “internal pressure” — the pressure measured by the console’s integrated sensor sited between the pump outlet and the oxygenator inlet. It is simply the pre-oxygenator pressure under the manufacturer’s name for it, and it is the highest pressure in the circuit — everything downstream (membrane, return tubing, cannula) is resistance the pump must overcome. Integrated systems typically report three: Pven (access, negative), Pint (pre-oxygenator) and Part (post-oxygenator, return limb). ΔP = Pint − Part — so Pint is one half of the transmembrane gradient, never interpreted alone.

Transmembrane pressure (TMP / ΔP)
TMP = Ppre-oxy − Ppost-oxy — the pressure drop across the fibre bundle as blood traverses it. Baseline is device- and flow-dependent (tens of mmHg); record it at your set flow. A rising TMP at fixed flow means clot is accumulating in the bundle (rising resistance) — the earliest and most specific sign of oxygenator thrombosis. Contrast with outflow obstruction, which raises pre- and post-oxy pressure together with ΔP unchanged (§01–§02). Interpret only at constant flow — ΔP also rises with flow, haematocrit and low temperature.
Post-oxygenator gases (post-oxy)
Post-oxy PaO₂ — sampled from the return-side (post-membrane) port at a known FdO₂. A healthy membrane gives ≈ 150–300 mmHg at FdO₂ 1.0. A falling post-oxy PaO₂ at fixed FdO₂ and flow = the membrane's oxygenation is failing (clot, plasma leak, condensate).
Post-oxy PaCO₂ / CO₂ removal — governed by sweep gas, not FdO₂. A rising PaCO₂ at fixed sweep with a normal ΔP suggests a water-logged (condensate) membrane — flush / brief high sweep; persistent failure = exchange.
Plasma leak — serosanguinous fluid weeping from the gas-exhaust port is membrane breakdown (plasma crossing into the gas phase). Gas exchange will follow it down — plan an exchange.
Haemolysis — mechanical & clot stress
Rising plasma-free haemoglobin (> 50, and certainly > 100 mg/dL), rising LDH, falling haptoglobin, haemoglobinuria and pink plasma point to pump-head or oxygenator thrombus, suckdown (high negative access pressure) or a malpositioned cannula. Trend LDH / pfHb as sensitive early markers.
Consumption — fibrinogen, platelets, D-dimer
Fibrinogen — a clotting circuit consumes it. A falling fibrinogen (trending down, especially < 1.5 g/L) with a rising D-dimer is ongoing intra-circuit clot — a consumptive, DIC-like picture of an oxygenator that is silently thrombosing. Replace to keep > 1.5 g/L (> 2 if bleeding) with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate — but a fibrinogen that keeps falling despite replacement is a circuit-change signal, not just a transfusion trigger.
Platelets & D-dimer — platelets are consumed by the circuit (keep > 50; > 80–100 peri-procedure). A rising D-dimer is an early, sensitive marker of intra-circuit fibrin turnover and often precedes the TMP rise. Falling fibrinogen + falling platelets + rising D-dimer together = consumption in a clotting circuit (exclude HIT). Check antithrombin — low AT causes heparin resistance, under-anticoagulation and more clot.
What to do about consumption
Two questions first: (1) is the fall consumption (circuit / DIC), dilution (massive transfusion / crystalloid), or loss (bleeding)? — D-dimer up + platelets down + fibrinogen down = consumption. (2) is anticoagulation adequate? Under-anticoagulation drives clot and consumption — check anti-Xa / ACT and antithrombin (low AT = heparin resistance).

Epicardial pacing — set-up, thresholds & troubleshooting

A Wire anatomy & the sensitivity paradox

Wires: atrial wires exit right of sternum, ventricular wires left (convention — always confirm). Bipolar = both to the box; unipolar = one wire + skin ground.
Sensitivity is inverse. A lower mV number = more sensitive box. Turn the number down to sense more (fix undersensing); up to sense less (fix oversensing).
Viva trap: a widely-circulated Southampton reference mislabels the sensing endpoint as the "pacing threshold." The sensing threshold is the largest mV at which the box still marks intrinsic beats — it is not the capture (pacing) threshold. Know both procedures cold.

Traps worth stating plainly
AAI with any block risk. The seductive mode after cardiac surgery — physiological, preserves conduction — and the one that kills if AV block develops, because nothing paces the ventricle. In a post-AVR or post-myectomy patient, choose DDD.
DDD tracking a fast atrium. AF or flutter in DDD drives ventricular pacing to the upper rate limit. If the ventricle suddenly paces fast and regularly at the upper rate, look at the atrial channel before you reach for a drug — switch to DDI.
Leaving VOO/DOO running. Asynchronous pacing competing with an intrinsic rhythm risks R-on-T and VF. Use it deliberately, then come off it.
VVI in a stiff or failing ventricle. Losing the atrial kick in RV failure, diastolic dysfunction or dynamic LVOTO can drop output sharply. If there is a P wave and wires, use it.
Pacing more than you need to. RV apical pacing is itself dyssynchronous. If native conduction is adequate, let it conduct — lengthen the AV delay or lower the rate rather than pacing the ventricle for no reason.
Crosstalk and the AV delay. In dual-chamber modes an atrial output sensed by the ventricular channel can inhibit ventricular pacing. Suspect it when ventricular pacing drops out in DDD; the blanking period and AV delay are where the fix lives (§F).

Module 07 · guided management

Hypotension & low output in dynamic LV outflow obstruction

A bedside guide for the hypotensive, low-output patient whose problem is dynamic obstruction of the LV outflow — from systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve, basal (septal) outflow obstruction, or both. The physiology inverts the usual shock reflexes: the instinctive moves (inotrope, vasodilate, restrict fluid) deepen the obstruction. Recognise it, then flip the management.

The one pattern to catch: hypotension / low output that worsens — or fails to respond — when you add an inotrope or pressor-inotrope. In a post-AVR, post-mitral-repair, hypovolaemic-hyperdynamic, septic or Takotsubo heart, that is dynamic LVOT obstruction until echo says otherwise. Confirm on echo before you invert.

1 The three mechanisms — one physiology

MechanismWhat obstructsEcho signature

Why it is dynamic: the gradient is set by cavity size and ejection velocity. A small, hyperdynamic, under-filled LV ejecting fast generates a Venturi effect that pulls the leaflet in and collapses the outflow — so anything that shrinks the cavity or speeds ejection (hypovolaemia, tachycardia, inotropes, vasodilatation) worsens it, and anything that enlarges the cavity or slows ejection (volume, afterload, β-blockade) relieves it.
Related patterns: mid-cavity and apical obstruction behave the same way — treat identically. A fixed subaortic membrane or valvular AS does not — that is a structural lesion and afterload / volume will not relieve it.

Bedside

Echo (point-of-care)

Don't be fooled: the SAM-related MR is dynamic and vanishes when you relieve the obstruction — treat the obstruction, not the “acute MR.” And a normal resting study does not exclude it — provoke (Valsalva, passive-leg-raise release, post-extrasystolic beat) if suspicion is high.

4 The management pathway

Step 0 · Stop the harm
Stop / wean inotropes (adrenaline, dobutamine, milrinone, dopamine) · stop vasodilators (GTN, ACE-inhibitor, milrinone's vasodilator effect) · stop over-diuresis. These are the moves deepening the obstruction.
Step 1 · Fill
Volume — fluid bolus (e.g. 250–500 mL crystalloid, repeat to response); passive leg raise as a test. A bigger LV cavity moves the mitral apparatus off the septum and lowers the gradient. Target generous, not dry, filling.
Step 2 · Raise afterload — pure vasoconstrictor
Phenylephrine is the cleanest choice — pure α, no inotropy or chronotropy (bolus 50–100 µg, or infusion). Noradrenaline or vasopressin are alternatives (vasopressin useful in vasoplegia). Higher aortic pressure raises LV end-systolic volume and splints the outflow open, cutting the gradient and the SAM-driven MR. Aim MAP ≥ 65 (higher if the gradient persists). Module 09 for rate ⇄ dose
Step 3 · Slow & synchronise
β-blockade — esmolol (titratable, ± load) or metoprolol — lowers contractility and lengthens diastolic filling, the two things that most reduce a dynamic gradient. Restore AV synchrony: cardiovert / rate-control AF, pace atrial or AV-sequential (the atrial kick fills the small stiff LV); avoid RV-only pacing, which loses the kick and can worsen the picture.
Step 4 · Reassess
Re-echo: is the gradient falling, the SAM resolving, the MR shrinking, the MAP holding? Titrate volume + vasoconstrictor + β-blocker to a resolving gradient, not to a single number.
Step 5 · Refractory obstruction
Deepen the same levers — confirm genuine euvolaemia-plus, more afterload, more β-blockade; cut sympathetic drive (analgesia / sedation). Disopyramide (negative inotrope) reduces the gradient in specialist hands. Treat the precipitant. Definitive: surgical relief (septal myectomy, revision of the mitral repair) or, in chronic HOCM, alcohol septal ablation.
Devices — mind the trap: an IABP is contraindicated — afterload reduction worsens the gradient. If mechanical support is unavoidable, prefer one that maintains or raises aortic pressure (peripheral VA-ECMO raises afterload); an LV-unloading device (Impella) has a nuanced, specialist-only role. Never reach for an inotrope-based device strategy.

No inotropes — adrenaline, dobutamine, milrinone, dopamine all deepen the gradient. A BP / lactate that worsens as you uptitrate is the clue you are on the wrong path.
No vasodilators — GTN, ACE-inhibitors and milrinone's vasodilator effect drop afterload and worsen obstruction.
No IABP — diastolic afterload reduction worsens a dynamic outflow gradient.
Don't run dry or fast — over-diuresis and tachycardia shrink the cavity and speed ejection; both feed the Venturi.

7 Bedside crash sequence

Recognise (worse on inotrope) → echo-confirm (SAM / dagger gradient) → STOP inotropes + vasodilators → FILL → phenylephrine (pure α) to raise MAP → β-block (esmolol) + restore AV synchrony → re-echo and titrate to a falling gradient → refractory: sedation, disopyramide, surgeon (myectomy / repair revision); not IABP, not inotropes.
Decision-support aid — confirm the diagnosis on echo, follow local protocol, and verify every drug dose independently (Module 09). Dynamic obstruction and its inverted management can harm if applied to a fixed structural lesion.

4 The perfusion layer — lactate, ScvO₂ and the CO₂ gap

These three answer the only question that matters in cardiogenic shock: is delivery matching demand? Read them together — each alone misleads.

Lactate — type A (hypoperfusion) vs type B (adrenaline/β-agonist glycolysis, metformin, liver, thiamine). A lactate that climbs as you escalate adrenaline is often type B — don't reflexively add flow the patient doesn't need. Clearance (≥ 10–20% per 2–4 h) is the goal, not a single value.
ScvO₂ / SvO₂ — the flow-to-demand balance. Low (< 60–65% / < 55–60%) = delivery inadequate: raise CO, Hb or SaO₂, or lower demand. High with a rising lactate = impaired extraction / shunt (sepsis, cyanide, microcirculatory failure) — or a wedged sampling catheter.
CO₂ gap (Pv-aCO₂) = central venous CO₂ − arterial CO₂. Normal < 6 mmHg (≈ 0.8 kPa). A widened gap is a flow problem — sluggish transit lets CO₂ accumulate. It is the most useful adjunct when the ScvO₂ looks reassuring: ScvO₂ > 70% with a wide CO₂ gap = the output is still inadequate for the metabolic demand, and is a strong argument to raise flow.

6 Correction — the ladder

The principle: fix delivery, not the number. In cardiogenic shock the acidosis is the readout of inadequate DO₂. Every rung below is subordinate to raising cardiac output and oxygen delivery — buffers treat the pH while the patient goes on dying of low flow.
Rung 1 · Restore flow and delivery the actual treatment
Optimise preload · pressors / inotropes by phenotype · rate and rhythm (AV synchrony) · relieve mechanical causes (tamponade, LVOTO, RVOT) · escalate to MCS when the ladder is exhausted — all per Module 01. Then raise the other DO₂ terms: Hb (transfuse if anaemic and shocked) and SaO₂. Reduce demand: sedation, analgesia, treat fever/shivering, take over the work of breathing.
Rung 2 · Ventilation — use it, but respect the heart
Increase alveolar ventilation to buy pH while the metabolic problem is fixed. But a metabolic acidosis with high minute-ventilation demand is a marker of severity — if you paralyse or over-sedate and let PaCO₂ rise to “normal”, the pH falls off a cliff. Match the patient's compensatory minute ventilation after intubation.
Hypercapnia raises PVR — in RV failure or pulmonary hypertension, permissive hypercapnia is not free; it can precipitate RV decompensation (Module 04). Conversely aggressive hyperventilation costs breath-stacking, high intrathoracic pressure and reduced venous return.
Rung 3 · Address the specific mechanism
Hyperchloraemic → switch to balanced crystalloid, stop the saline/chloride load. Ketoacidosis → insulin + glucose. Toxic → antidote. Renal failure with accumulating acid → RRT. Contraction alkalosis → chloride and potassium replacement ± acetazolamide. Citrate accumulation → reduce/stop citrate. Thiamine for unexplained lactataemia.
Rung 4 · Buffer — last, and narrowly
See §7. Bicarbonate is a temporising measure for severe acidaemia, not a treatment for shock.
Rung 5 · Extracorporeal clearance
RRT corrects acidosis reliably (bicarbonate-buffered) and removes the acid load in renal failure — a better answer than repeated bicarbonate boluses in the anuric, volume-overloaded, acidotic patient. ECCO₂R / the ECMO circuit clears CO₂ via sweep gas when ventilation cannot.

7 Sodium bicarbonate — the honest position

Where it is defensible
· Severe acidaemia, conventionally pH < 7.10–7.20, especially with haemodynamic instability or catecholamine unresponsiveness
· Normal-gap / bicarbonate-losing acidosis (GI, renal, hyperchloraemic) — here you are genuinely replacing lost buffer
· Hyperkalaemia · sodium-channel-blocker toxicity (TCA) · severe acidosis with AKI while arranging RRT (BICAR-ICU signalled benefit in the AKIN 2–3 subgroup)
Why it usually doesn't help
· Generates CO₂ — worsens intracellular and CNS acidosis unless ventilation can clear it
· Drops ionised calcium — directly reduces contractility and vascular tone
· Sodium and volume load · hypernatraemia · hyperosmolality
· Left-shifts the oxyhaemoglobin curve — impairs tissue O₂ offloading
· Rebound alkalosis once the lactate is metabolised
· No mortality benefit in lactic acidosis of shock — it treats the number
If you give it: only in a patient whose ventilation can clear the CO₂ generated; give it as a considered dose with a defined endpoint, re-gas early, and watch ionised calcium (replace it). THAM is an alternative that buffers without generating CO₂ — useful when hypercapnia is the limiting factor — but is contraindicated in anuria and can cause hypoglycaemia and hyperkalaemia.
Thresholds and doses are unit- and patient-specific — follow local protocol and verify every dose independently (Module 09).

8 The traps

Correcting CO₂ too fast — on ECMO initiation or after intubation of a chronically hypercapnic patient, a rapid PaCO₂ fall causes cerebral vasoconstriction (and, with a chronic buffer, a swing to alkalaemia). Reduce PaCO₂ gradually — start the sweep low and titrate.
Chasing a normal pH with bicarbonate while cardiac output stays low — the commonest and most harmful error on this page.
Hyperoxia — a beautiful PaO₂ is not a goal; excess O₂ causes vasoconstriction, oxidative injury and worse post-arrest outcomes. Target normoxia and wean FiO₂ / sweep accordingly.
Sampling errors — wrong site on VA-ECMO (§04 ECMO), air bubbles or delayed analysis (falsely low CO₂, high O₂), heparin dilution, a wedged catheter for the “venous” sample, or a limb distal to an obstructed cannula.
VBG ≠ ABG — venous pH and HCO₃ track arterial acceptably for trend, but venous PaCO₂ runs higher and venous PO₂ tells you nothing about oxygenation. In shock the arteriovenous difference widens, so trend arterial gases when it matters.
Forgetting the co-factors — ionised calcium, potassium (shifts with pH: correcting acidosis drops K⁺), magnesium, phosphate and thiamine all move with acid–base management and all affect the myocardium.

9 Bedside sequence

Read — oxygenation → pH → PaCO₂ → HCO₃/BE → compensation → albumin-corrected gap + delta ratio → lactate / ScvO₂ / CO₂ gap. Name the primary disorder and every co-existing one. Ask “is this a delivery problem?” — if yes, raise flow (Module 01) and re-gas. Fix the specific mechanism (chloride load, ketones, renal, citrate, diuretic). Support ventilation to match the compensatory demand, respecting PVR. Buffer only for severe acidaemia with an endpoint, watching ionised calcium. Re-gas and trend — lactate clearance and closing base deficit are the endpoints.
Decision-support aid — confirm against local protocol and the full clinical picture; formulae are guides, and a gas is never interpreted apart from the patient in front of you.
